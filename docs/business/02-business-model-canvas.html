Block-by-block reasoning

Customer segments — the beachhead is paper, not software

The sharpest framing is not "kirana stores". It is shops that bill on paper today. A shop already running Vyapar has absorbed the typing cost and has data to migrate; a shop with a calculator and a notebook has neither. Ragas Coffee — 24 SKUs, single counter — is the shape. Tier 1 must cover cafés and kirana alike, which is why the tier is named for its counter count, not its trade.

Value propositions — one claim is hard, the rest are projections

The capex comparison is a hard cost fact and survives contact with numbers if stated carefully — see the competitive note, where the honest denominator is WeP's ₹9,990 billing machine, not their ₹82,790 terminal bundle. The literacy argument is the one that is genuinely differentiating and genuinely unproven: it predicts that a shop can put someone at the counter who could not have run a typing POS. Nobody but the founder has yet spoken to this system. Proving that with one non-founder operator is worth more than any deck slide.

Key resources — where a moat could actually come from

Not the voice technology. Sarvam is a vendor and on-device ASR will commoditise. The defensible asset is the per-shop alias catalog: after a month, a shop's system knows that this shop's "aloo" is that SKU at that price. That is switching cost the shop built themselves, and it is invisible to a competitor quoting a lower price. Second is the corrected-utterance corpus — the only realistic route to an on-device model tuned to code-mixed retail speech, which is in turn the only route to ₹0 marginal inference. The moat and the cost structure are the same asset. That is the strategic core of this business and the deck should say so.

Cost structure — the number that decides everything

Every other block is ordinary SaaS. This one is not. A ₹199/month subscription leaves roughly ₹150 after platform costs to cover ~24,000 utterances, support and CAC amortisation. No cloud ASR clears that. Three routes exist: on-device ASR (the plan, and what the corpus is for), radically fewer cloud calls (wake-word gating — already the architecture), or a higher entry price (which forfeits the wedge). An investor who does not probe this is not paying attention; one who does should be met with the roadmap, not a deflection.

Revenue — and the honest note on data monetisation

Aggregated insight to FMCG brands is the line most likely to be over-promised. It requires scale we do not have, consent we have not designed, and it sits uncomfortably against the shop's own interest — a brand paying for shelf intelligence is not paying to help the shopkeeper negotiate. Keep it as an opt-in option late in the model, never as a load-bearing revenue assumption. ASSUMPTION — VALIDATE

Key partners — Sarvam is a dependency, not a partnership

Stated plainly because diligence will: the product's core capability is currently bought from a single vendor at a price that does not close the unit economics. That is acceptable for a prototype and unacceptable as a destination, which is exactly why on-device ASR is the first funded engineering hire.

Where RAGaaS fits

Per the founder's direction: VBS is the wedge and owns the counter; RAGaaS supplies the CRM layer. The clean division is that VBS knows what was sold and to whom; RAGaaS knows the conversation with that customer. Shared customer identity — the mobile number already captured at the counter — is the join. This is a credible expansion story precisely because it does not require VBS to become a CRM: it requires one integration against a product that already exists. Keep it as a module in the architecture and one slide in the deck, not as the headline.
